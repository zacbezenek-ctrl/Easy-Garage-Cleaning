How to Get a Tax Deduction When Donating Your Junk (IRS Rules Explained)

By Zac Bezenek April 28, 2026 8 min read
Fort Collins garage guide

By Zac Bezenek · Easy Garage CleaningPublished 2026-01-183 min read

This article is for general information purposes only. It is not tax advice. Consult a CPA or tax professional for guidance on your specific situation.

When you donate items during a garage cleanout — appliances, furniture, tools, household goods — there's real tax value there if you know how to document it properly. The IRS has specific rules about what qualifies, how to value non-cash donations, and what paperwork you need. Here's how it actually works.

First: Do You Itemize?

Charitable donation deductions only matter if you itemize deductions on your federal tax return (Schedule A) rather than taking the standard deduction. For 2026, the standard deduction is $15,000 for single filers and $30,000 for married filing jointly. If your total itemizable deductions — including mortgage interest, state and local taxes, and charitable donations — don't exceed the standard deduction, the donation deduction won't change your tax bill.

For many homeowners in Fort Collins, especially those who own their homes and pay significant property taxes and mortgage interest, itemizing can make sense. But if you're renting or your mortgage is largely paid off, you may be under the standard deduction threshold. Check with your tax preparer.

That said: even if you're not sure, getting the donation receipt costs you nothing. Keep it, and let your CPA decide whether it applies.

To deduct a non-cash donation, three things must be true:

  1. The recipient organization is a qualified 501(c)(3). Habitat for Humanity ReStore, ARC Thrift, and Goodwill all qualify. Donating to a neighbor, a church rummage sale run by a for-profit, or an individual does not qualify — even if it feels charitable.
  2. The items are in "good used condition or better." The IRS explicitly allows deductions only for items in good or better condition. Broken, heavily damaged, or non-functional items technically don't qualify (though a professional appraisal can sometimes unlock deductions for single items worth over $500).
  3. You have documentation. More on this below — the paperwork requirements vary by donation amount.

How to Value Your Donations

Unlike cash donations, you — the donor — assign the value to non-cash donations. The IRS standard is fair market value: what a willing buyer would pay a willing seller for the item in its current condition at a thrift store. This is not what you paid for it originally, not what it costs new, and not a sentimental estimate.

The IRS publishes Publication 561 (Determining the Value of Donated Property) for guidance. Goodwill also publishes a donation valuation guide on their website that gives reasonable ranges for common items. These are the figures to use.

Here are realistic fair market value ranges for common cleanout donations:

Item Typical FMV Range Notes
Sofa / couch $50 – $250 Depends heavily on condition and style
Dining table with chairs $75 – $300 Solid wood commands higher end
Dresser / chest of drawers $25 – $150 Particle board at low end
Working refrigerator $75 – $200 Age and size matter
Washer or dryer (working) $75 – $175 each Pair worth slightly more together
Power tools (working) $25 – $150 Brand and condition vary widely
Hand tools (lot) $25 – $100 Per substantial lot, not per item
Bags of clothing $10 – $30 per bag Standard estimate per bag of mixed clothing
Kitchen cabinets (set) $100 – $400 ReStore sells these — often significant value
Bicycle (adult, working) $25 – $125 Brand matters; beat-up kids' bikes at the low end

Be honest and reasonable. Don't value a 15-year-old sofa with worn upholstery at $300. The IRS can audit non-cash donation deductions, and overvalued claims are a red flag.

The Receipt Rules: What You Need

Under $250 in a single contribution

A simple receipt from the organization showing the name, date, and a description of items donated. A cash register receipt or handwritten slip from the donation center qualifies. Keep this with your tax documents.

$250 or more in a single contribution

You must have a written acknowledgment from the organization that includes: the organization's name, the date of the contribution, a description of the property donated, and a statement of whether the organization provided any goods or services in exchange. A basic donation receipt from Habitat ReStore or ARC meets this requirement. The key is getting it before you file — the IRS won't let you reconstruct documentation after the fact.

Over $500 total non-cash donations for the year

You must complete IRS Form 8283 (Noncash Charitable Contributions) and attach it to your return. Section A covers items $500–$5,000. You fill out descriptions, dates, values, and the organization that received them.

Single item or group over $5,000

Requires a qualified appraisal by a certified appraiser. This threshold is rarely hit in a typical garage cleanout, but can come up with high-value art, antiques, or collectibles.

Putting It Together: A Typical Cleanout Example

A typical Easy Garage Cleaning job with donations might look like this:

  • Working refrigerator → ReStore: FMV $125
  • Dining table and 4 chairs → ReStore: FMV $150
  • Dresser → ReStore: FMV $60
  • Power drill + tools → ReStore: FMV $85
  • 4 bags of clothing → ARC: FMV $80
  • Total donated value: $500

If you're in the 22% federal tax bracket, a $500 deduction saves you $110 in taxes. Not life-changing, but $110 you didn't have to do anything extra for — we handle the drop-off and bring you the receipt.

Frequently Missed Details

  • You cannot deduct items donated to individuals — even a neighbor in financial hardship. Must go to a qualified 501(c)(3).
  • The deduction is capped at 60% of your adjusted gross income for cash and most property donations to public charities. This cap rarely affects garage cleanout donations, but matters for large estate donations.
  • Keep photographs of donated items. If you're ever audited, visual evidence of condition and quantity supports your claimed values.
  • Colorado has no separate state deduction for charitable contributions — Colorado follows federal AGI, so the deduction flows through automatically if you itemize federally.
